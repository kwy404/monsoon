      <p class="mx-auto mt-6 max-w-2xl text-lg text-muted-foreground text-pretty sm:text-xl">Every second Saturday, a church hall in Harrowgate fixes about ninety broken things for free. I spent a year counting what that is actually worth.</p>

        <div class="prose prose-lg font-serif">
          <p>The first thing you notice is the smell: solder, burnt dust and strong tea. The second is the queue. By ten past nine on a wet Saturday there are already fourteen people holding things they were told were not worth fixing.</p>
          <h2 id="saturday">A Saturday in the hall</h2>
          <p>The Harrowgate repair cafe runs on four folding tables and a rota of eleven volunteers. One table does electrics, one does textiles, one does bicycles and the fourth is called <em>everything else</em>, which on my first visit included a cuckoo clock, a child's scooter and a coffee grinder that had been dropped down a flight of stairs.</p>
          <p>Nobody pays. There is a jar for donations and a sign asking visitors to stay and watch their item being opened. That second rule turns out to matter more than the first.</p>
          <blockquote>People do not come for the free fix. They come because someone finally explains why the thing broke.</blockquote>
          <h2 id="counting">Counting what gets saved</h2>
          <p>Repair cafes are easy to love and hard to measure. Organizers report items fixed, but rarely what those items would have cost to replace, or how long they keep working afterwards. So for twelve sessions I kept a spreadsheet.</p>
          <h3 id="method">How I measured</h3>
          <ul>
            <li>Every item was logged at the door with its category and the owner's estimate of a replacement price.</li>
            <li>Volunteers marked each item as fixed, partly fixed or not fixable, with the time spent.</li>
            <li>Three months later I phoned a sample of owners to ask whether the item was still in use.</li>
          </ul>
          <h3 id="numbers">The numbers</h3>
          <p>Across 1,084 items, 63 percent left the hall working. Small kitchen appliances had the best odds, and laptops the worst, mostly because of glued batteries and parts that are no longer sold.</p>

        <div class="prose prose-lg font-serif">
          <p>Using the owners' own replacement prices, the hall saved its visitors roughly <strong>&pound;31,400</strong> over the year, against running costs of about &pound;2,100 for rent, spare fuses and biscuits. At the three month follow up, 88 percent of repaired items were still in daily use.</p>
          <h2 id="skills">Skills are the real stock</h2>
          <p>The money is the headline, but it undersells the hall. Volunteers estimate that one visitor in five comes back the next month to fix something themselves at the table, with supervision. Several now volunteer.</p>
        </div>

        <aside class="callout callout-primary my-8">
          <p class="callout-title"><svg viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" aria-hidden="true"><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="10"/><path d="M12 16v-4M12 8h.01"/></svg> Start one near you</p>
          <p>You need a room, public liability insurance, a few tools and one person who can safely test mains electrics. The rest, organizers say, turns up on its own.</p>
        </aside>

        <div class="prose prose-lg font-serif">
          <h2 id="next">What would scale</h2>
          <p>The hall cannot fix a laptop whose battery is glued in place, and no amount of goodwill changes that. What would help is boring: spare parts sold for ten years, screws instead of glue and service manuals that are not secret. Several of those rules now exist on paper. The repair cafe is where you find out whether they work.</p>
          <p>As I packed up on my last Saturday, a retired postman named Clive held up a radio he had owned since 1979. It crackled, then played the shipping forecast. The whole room clapped.</p>
